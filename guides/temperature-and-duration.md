Two numbers define a session: the temperature of the oil and how long the stream runs. Both
are adjusted to the presentation.

## Temperature

The target is comfortably above body temperature, not hot. The classical instruction is that
the oil should be *sukhoshna*, pleasantly warm. In practice:

| Presentation | Working range | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Vata-predominant | 38 to 40 °C | Warm, grounding |
| Pitta-predominant | 36 to 38 °C | Barely above body heat, cooling in effect |
| Kapha-predominant | 40 to 42 °C | Distinctly warm, mobilising |

Test on the inner wrist, not the fingertips. Oil that reads as sharp or stinging is too hot,
and the forehead is more sensitive than the hand.

The practical difficulty is that oil cools while it runs. Over a 40-minute session an
unheated vessel will drop several degrees. Traditional practice handles this by working with
an assistant who reheats and swaps oil; modern practice handles it either the same way, with
a warmed pot, or with a heated reservoir.

## Duration

Session lengths in common practice:

| Presentation | Typical pour | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vata-predominant | 40 to 45 minutes | The longest, to allow the state to settle |
| Pitta-predominant | 30 to 40 minutes | Moderate |
| Kapha-predominant | 25 to 30 minutes | Shortest, to avoid heaviness |

Courses are usually run as a series rather than a single session, commonly 7 to 14
consecutive days in a clinical Panchakarma setting, or weekly in a spa context.

## Why the stream must stay unbroken

The continuity is the point. An intermittent stream produces a startle response each time it
resumes, which works against the settling the practice is aiming at. This is the single
hardest part to maintain and the main reason the traditional setup uses a suspended vessel
with a controlled orifice rather than pouring by hand.

## Volume

A session uses considerably more oil than people expect, because the oil is recirculated
rather than consumed. How much depends on the apparatus, and the two numbers in circulation
are often quoted as though they were one.

A traditional dhara patra, filled and refilled by hand, runs on roughly half a litre to a
litre. A closed-loop vessel holds more by design, because it has to keep a continuous stream
running without a refill: the Surya Shirodhara takes about 1.5 litres per session, three
500 ml bottles, in a two litre vessel.

Either way almost all of it is recovered. The loss per session is what soaks into hair, cloth
and the catch basin, not what is poured.
