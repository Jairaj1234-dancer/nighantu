**Disclosure.** This site is published by Age Ayurveda, which sells a Shirodhara vessel and
the oils used with it. This page sets out what published standards require. It is not a
specification for any product, ours included, and where a standard is silent we say so rather
than fill the gap with a recommendation.

A procurement or clinical governance conversation asks different questions from a spa's. Not
what the treatment is, but what the rules say, who may do it, and what the unit has to contain.
This page collects what we could actually verify against primary documents, and is explicit
about the places where the commonly repeated figures do not have the provenance they are
usually given.

## A caution about the numbers everyone quotes

The area and equipment figures below circulate widely, on consulting and clinic sites, as
"CCIM norms". We went looking for that source and could not reach a live one: the
NCISM and CCIM college regulation those citations point to is no longer retrievable.

The only current, readable government document we found containing these exact figures is
**Development of Minimum Standards of Clinical Establishments, Ayurveda**, published by the
Ministry of Health and Family Welfare under the Clinical Establishments (Registration and
Regulation) Act 2010. That document is itself marked *Draft*, and its own introduction says
its method is based on CCIM norms, IPHS guidelines and the Gujarat MSR for Ayurvedic
hospitals.

So the figures are real and they come from a government document, but they are a draft
standard derived from CCIM norms rather than the CCIM regulation itself. We cite it as what it
is. If you are making a capital decision, get the current requirement from your own state
authority rather than from this page or from anyone quoting "CCIM norms" without a link.

## What the unit has to contain

From the Ministry of Health draft standard cited above.

**Area, by bed strength:**

| Hospital size | Panchakarma unit area |
|---|---|
| 100 beds and above | 2000 sq ft |
| 51 to 100 beds | approximately 1000 sq ft |
| Under 50 beds | approximately 500 sq ft |

**Rooms, for hospitals above 100 beds.** The standard lists fourteen spaces, and it separates
them by sex rather than by procedure alone: Snehana room male and female, Swedana room male and
female, **Shirodhara room male and female**, Vamana room male and female, Virechana room male
and female, Basti room male and female, a Panchakarma therapist room, and a store room.

Shirodhara is named in its own right here, not folded into a general Panchakarma heading. That
matters for planning, because it means a compliant unit at that scale is providing two rooms
for this procedure alone.

**Equipment.** The same document's Snehana Karma annexure lists, for Shirodhara: two
Shirodhara yantras, two stands, two dharapatras. Its furniture annexure gives a massage table
of 7 ft by 2.5 ft in wood or fibre, optional for a hospital under 10 beds and one per procedure
room above that.

## Who may perform it

Two different documents answer two different halves of this.

**Supervision.** NABH's *Accreditation Standards for Panchakarma Clinics*, 1st edition,
February 2017, states at COP.5(i) that Panchakarma therapies are done only under the guidance
of a vaidya. Its MOM.4(c) interpretation adds that this shall be someone who at a minimum
holds a recognised qualification in Ayurveda. Its glossary defines vaidya simply as a
physician.

**Delivery.** The person performing the procedure has a defined vocational qualification. The
Healthcare Sector Skill Council's **Panchakarma Technician** qualification pack is
**HSS/Q3601**, version 3.0, at **NSQF level 4**, approved at the 23rd NSQC meeting on 22 August
2019. It runs to 1500 hours: 400 theory, 600 practical, 500 on the job. Its compulsory units
cover preparing for a session, carrying out therapy as prescribed, post-therapy procedures, a
safe and secure environment, and biomedical waste and infection control.

Shirodhara is not a standalone competency unit within it. It appears throughout the assessment
equipment list, a Shirodhara yantra, an automated Shirodhara stand, a kindi for pouring, while
the performance criteria address Panchakarma administration generally.

## Accreditation, and which standard applies

NABH accreditation in India is **voluntary**. It is not a licensing requirement and not a
condition of offering the therapy.

Which standard applies turns on whether patients stay overnight. The Panchakarma Clinic
standard is explicit about its own scope: a Panchakarma clinic should provide services that do
not require an overnight stay, and Panchakarma requiring an overnight stay under medical
observation falls under the hospital accreditation standards instead.

There is also a newer *Accreditation Standards for AYUSH Hospitals*, 1st edition, dated
September 2024, and an older AYUSH hospital accreditation programme run with the Ministry of
AYUSH. The copy of the 2024 standard we retrieved was watermarked *Draft* throughout, and we
could not establish whether it supersedes the older programme or sits alongside it. Check the
current position with NABH directly before relying on either.

Both NABH standards treat Shirodhara as an example of a Panchakarma procedure rather than as a
separately accredited thing. What they require of it is what they require of Panchakarma
generally: written guidance, informed consent, and protocols to prevent adverse events.

## Where the standards are silent

**Oil reuse between patients is not addressed.** We read the infection control chapters of both
NABH standards directly, IC.1 to IC.4 in the Panchakarma Clinic standard and IPC.1 to IPC.8 in
the AYUSH hospital draft. Both cover hand hygiene, cleaning and disinfection of instruments,
equipment and devices, biomedical waste, linen and laundry, engineering controls and
housekeeping. Neither mentions therapeutic oil, its reuse, or single versus multiple patient
use anywhere.

That is a real gap, and it is worth naming rather than papering over. A unit offering
Shirodhara has to write its own policy on how many sessions a charge of oil serves and whether
it is ever carried between patients, and be able to show it. Our own practice, stated as
practice and not as a standard, is a maximum of three sessions on a charge of oil with
straining after each, and no carrying between people.

One limit on that finding: we checked the national standards. A state AYUSH department circular
could conceivably address it, and we have not read all of those. If you know of one, please
[tell us](/corrections/) and we will correct this page.

## What this means for equipment choice

The standards above assume a fixed installation: a dedicated room, a dharapatra and stand, a
droni. That is the right arrangement for an accredited inpatient unit and we would not argue
otherwise.

A self-contained vessel is a different case and it is worth being straight about which. It
suits an outpatient clinic that cannot commit a plumbed room, a unit adding the procedure
before committing capital to it, a department that needs the therapy to move between rooms, or
a satellite or camp setting. It does not replace a dhara room in a hospital building one.
[Choosing equipment](/shirodhara/choosing-equipment/) compares the actual devices including
where ours loses, and [medical device status](/shirodhara/medical-device-status/) covers the
regulatory classification question a procurement team will raise.
