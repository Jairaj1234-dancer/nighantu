## The actual choice: powered or gravity

Every Shirodhara device is one of two things.

**A powered machine** holds a reservoir of liquid, heats it with an element, and pumps it to a
vessel above the forehead. Because it reheats continuously, the temperature at minute forty is
the temperature at minute one. Several models also oscillate the vessel so the stream tracks
slowly across the forehead, and most add a timer.

**A gravity vessel** is the classical apparatus: a pot with a small orifice, suspended about ten
to twelve centimetres above the forehead, flow governed by head pressure and the orifice, often
with a wick to keep the stream from breaking into drips. The operator warms the oil before the
session and the run-off is caught and returned, either by an assistant or by a closed-loop base.

The trade is straightforward. Powered machines solve temperature drift, which is the single
hardest problem in a solo session. Gravity vessels need no electricity, have nothing electronic
to fail, and are the object the classical texts actually describe.

### What temperature drift really costs you

Oil cools while it runs. Over a forty minute session an unheated vessel can fall several degrees,
which takes a Vata-appropriate 39 °C down towards a temperature that no longer reads as warm.
Traditional practice solved this with a second person swapping and reheating oil. If you work
alone and run long sessions, a heated reservoir is the feature you are paying for, and the rest
is packaging.

If you have an assistant, or you run shorter Kapha-style sessions, or you warm to the top of the
range and accept the drift, the problem is much smaller and so is the case for a machine.

### Things buyers underestimate

**Oil or water.** The classical practice is oil. Some powered units are designed primarily for
water, with oil available as an optional conversion. Oil is viscous, it degrades pumps and
tubing, and it is far harder to flush out. Check what a device is natively built for.

**Cleaning.** Every pump, heater and length of tubing that touches oil has to be cleaned between
clients. A machine with an internal circuit is more to clean than a pot and a basin, and this is
recurring labour you will do hundreds of times.

**Electrical compliance if you export or resell.** A device with a heater and a pump, sold for a
therapy context, brings electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility requirements with it,
and in the EU raises the question of whether it is a medical device. A purely mechanical vessel
carries none of that. If you plan to resell across borders, this is a real cost difference and
not a detail.

**Claims.** Marketing in this category varies a great deal in how carefully it is worded. Some
sellers advertise Shirodhara equipment against named conditions. Whether or not those claims are
defensible where the seller is based, repeating them in your own store or clinic makes them your
claims in your market. Read what you are about to republish.

## How to choose

**You run a clinic with mains power and back-to-back clients.** A powered machine with a heated
reservoir and a timer earns its price in consistency and in one less thing to think about during
a session. The oscillating models are worth trying before buying, because not every school of
practice uses an oscillating stream.

**You work alone and want long sessions.** Temperature stability is your binding constraint.
Either a powered unit, or a gravity setup with closed-loop recovery so returned oil re-enters
the vessel still warm, plus a decision to warm to the top of the range at the start.

**You want the classical practice, or you work where power is not guaranteed.** A gravity vessel
is the right object and not a compromise. Retreats, outdoor and off-grid settings, and places
with unreliable supply all point the same way. So does the case where you simply do not want a
heater and a pump running beside a person covered in oil.

**You are curious and want to try it at home a few times.** Start with a session at a clinic
before buying anything, so you know what a correct session feels like. Then the honest advice is
that the cheapest workable setup is a suspended vessel with a controlled orifice, a basin, and a
water bath to warm the oil. See [equipment and setup](../equipment-and-setup/) for what a
working arrangement actually needs.

**You are reselling or exporting.** Weigh the electrical compliance point above, the cost of
returning a heavy powered unit for warranty service, and whether you can support a device with
electronics in it from wherever you are.

## Where our own device loses

We sell the Surya Shirodhara, so this section exists to be useful rather than flattering.

It is **fully manual**. Oil temperature and flow are set by hand and there is no thermostat, so
across a long session the temperature drifts downward unless you intervene. Every powered machine
listed above holds temperature better than it does. It has **no timer**. It does **not
oscillate**. At around 3.5 kg it is **heavier than the lightest powered unit** on this page. Our
warranty is **replacement-parts-first with no full-unit return**, which is honest given return
freight from India but is weaker on paper than a two year parts and labour guarantee.

If a thermostat, a timer and an oscillating stream are what you want, buy a powered machine. One
of the ones above will suit you better than ours will.

What it is good at is narrower and real: it is a one litre closed-loop steel and brass alloy
vessel on a collapsible frame that runs on gravity alone, so it needs no power, no plumbing, no
drainage and no fit-out, and it is built natively for oil rather than converted to it. It is the
classical apparatus made portable. For the buyer who chose Shirodhara because it is a traditional
practice, that is the argument. For the buyer who wants a machine, it is not.
