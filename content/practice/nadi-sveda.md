## What it is

Nāḍī sveda is sudation delivered through a nāḍī, a tube. Instead of putting the whole body in a steam chamber, the vapour rising off a herbal decoction is led along a tube and played over one region. The record classes it as a pūrva-karma, meaning it belongs to the preparatory stage that precedes the main pañcakarma procedures rather than being an end in itself, and files it under the subcategory nāḍī.

## Where it comes from in the classical literature

The record cites Caraka Sūtra 14, and that citation checks out. Sūtrasthāna 14 is the Svedādhyāya, the chapter on sudation. Verses 39 and 40 enumerate the thirteen fire-based varieties, with nāḍī among them, and verse 43 describes the method in unusual physical detail.

The translation has the tube made "of the shape of the elephant's trunk" from the leaves of bamboo, Indian beech or mudar, one vyāma (about six feet) or half a vyāma long, with two or three bends along its course. The drugs are decocted in a pot with the lid closed tightly "so as not to allow the steam to escape", and the patient takes the steam from the apparatus. The reason given for the bends is worth reading closely: the steam, "passing through the bends in a tortuous course, is deprived of its violence and without irritating the skin, it sweats the patient with ease". Temperature control is designed into the instrument.

On ingredients the chapter is broad rather than specific. It speaks of root, fruit, leaves and buds of the vegetable group, or the flesh and heads of birds and beasts of hot potency, combined as needed with sour, salt and unctuous articles, or with urine, milk and similar substances. It gives no duration for nāḍī sveda.

## What it involves

The record gives three steps: steam directed by an insulated tube to a specific area, a decoction of Daśamūla or Sahacara as the source of the vapour, and a time of fifteen to twenty-five minutes.

The fifteen to twenty-five minute figure is the record's, not Caraka's; the classical chapter does not time the procedure.

## What is used

The record's dravya list names three: Daśamūla, Sahacara and Nirguṇḍī. Nirguṇḍī appears only in that list, not in the procedure steps, so it is presented as an option rather than as part of the described method. No oils and no materials are recorded, and the insulated tube is the only instrument named.

Where the record narrows to three named dravyas, Caraka names a whole class of hot-potency plant and animal materials. The two are not in conflict, but the record's list is a modern shortlist rather than a classical prescription.

## What to expect and what to ask

The record marks this supervised, so it is set up and run by someone trained. What the record does not give is a course length, a frequency, a season, any contraindication, or any adverse event.

The classical description makes temperature the thing to ask about, since the whole geometry of the tube exists to take the force out of the steam before it reaches skin. Reasonable questions are how the steam temperature is regulated, how the skin is checked during the fifteen to twenty-five minutes, which decoction is being used and why, and how many sessions are proposed.
