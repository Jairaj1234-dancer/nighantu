## What it is

Vātātapika is not a formulation. It is a mode of delivery, one of the two ways Caraka says rasāyana can be administered.

In kuṭīpraveśika, the person enters a purpose-built cottage and stays confined there for the duration of the therapy; Caraka's chapter gives instructions for constructing that cottage. In vātātapika, named for vāta (wind) and ātapa (sun), the same category of therapy is administered to someone who remains exposed to the weather and carries on. The source record puts it directly: the patient continues normal activities outdoors.

This is the mode most people who take a rasāyana today are actually in, whether or not the term is used.

## Where it comes from in the classical literature

The record cites two chapters. The first checks out cleanly: Caraka Cikitsāsthāna 1.1, the Abhaya-Āmalakīya Rasāyana Pāda, is where the two-fold division is stated, that rasāyana is of two types, kuṭīpraveśika and vātātapika, the latter administered even when the individual is exposed to wind and sun.

The second is a rasāyana chapter but not a source for this distinction. Suśruta Cikitsāsthāna 27 is the Sarvopaghātaśamanīya Rasāyana Adhyāya, confirmed as a chapter on rejuvenation therapy. The translation consulted does not present the kuṭīpraveśika and vātātapika division at all; it mentions a pre-built cottage in connection with one specific formulation and otherwise does not discuss the two modes. It is a reasonable general citation for rasāyana and a poor one for vātātapika specifically.

## What it involves

The record describes a single-dravya rasāyana given with an appropriate anupāna (the vehicle or carrier the medicine is taken with), while the person continues normal outdoor activity. The record sets the duration at thirty days.

That thirty-day figure is the record's, not Caraka's. The chapter consulted states no overall course duration for rasāyana therapy; the only day-counts it gives concern a short preparatory phase of three, five or seven days after purification and before the rasāyana proper.

## What is used

The record names four single dravyas: Aśvagandhā, Āmalakī, Brāhmī and Guḍūcī, each with an appropriate anupāna.

These are practice-tradition choices rather than the ones the cited chapters foreground. Caraka Cikitsā 1.1 is built around abhayā (harītakī) and āmalakī, so āmalakī is on solid ground there. The single-herb rasāyanas listed in the Suśruta chapter, as rendered in the translation consulted, are viḍaṅga, gambhārī, balā, vārāhīkanda, vijayasāra and śāṇa, none of which is in the record's list.

## Who it is for and who should avoid it

Both cited chapters gate rasāyana behind conditions, and they are the reason this sits at supervised level.

Caraka Cikitsā 1.1 states that the person should first be cleansed by pañcakarma, and that rejuvenation therapy is administered afterwards, once he is happy and has regained his strength. Suśruta Cikitsā 27 is blunter still: it is not beneficial to give rasāyana to a person who has not been purified, and the chapter's image is that rasāyana will not bestow good qualities on an unpurified person just as a dirty canvas does not catch colour. The same chapter restricts rasāyana to young and middle-aged persons.

Caraka's chapter also notes cautions attached to harītakī specifically, including indigestion and emaciation from excess alcohol or sexual indulgence.

## What to ask before booking

Whether the practitioner considers a purification step necessary first, given that both cited chapters treat it as a precondition; which single dravya is proposed, in what form, and with what anupāna; how long the course is intended to run and where that figure comes from; and how any regular medication you take is being accounted for, since a thirty-day daily regimen is long enough for interactions to matter.
