Seven herbs on this site have published reports of liver injury in people who took them:
[Giloy](/herb/guduchi/) (*Tinospora cordifolia*), [ashwagandha](/herb/ashwagandha/),
[turmeric](/herb/turmeric/) and curcumin supplements, [bakuchi](/herb/bakuchi/) (*Psoralea
corylifolia*), *Garcinia cambogia* (a relative of [vrikshamla](/herb/vrikshamla/), *Garcinia indica*), oral
[aloe vera](/herb/aloe-vera/) and [senna](/herb/senna/). The NIH LiverTox database, which
grades every substance it reviews from A (well established cause of liver injury) to E
(unlikely cause), rates
[Tinospora](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK608429/) and
[turmeric](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548561/) A,
[ashwagandha](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/),
[Garcinia](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548087/) and
[aloe vera](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548634/) B, and
[senna](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547922/) D.

Two things have to be said together. Most people who take these herbs do not develop liver
injury: LiverTox puts the frequency for Garcinia at under 1 in 10,000 users and gives no
figure for the others, because nobody has one. And the injury, when it happens, can be
serious: the published series include deaths and liver transplants, and in the Indian series
these fell almost entirely on people who already had chronic liver disease. The reports below show association, assessed with
causality tools such as RUCAM, not proof; many involved multi-ingredient products, other
medicines or possible adulteration, and the authors say so.

The two review papers that collect this literature for Indian herbs are
[Philips 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33033566/) in the *World Journal of
Hepatology*, and [Philips and Theruvath 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38640296/)
in *Medicine*, which names *Tinospora cordifolia*, *Withania somnifera*, *Curcuma longa* and
*Psoralea corylifolia* as the herbs with the clearest signal and *Centella asiatica*
([Mandukparni](/herb/mandukparni/)), *Garcinia cambogia*, *Cassia angustifolia* and
*Morinda citrifolia* as ones with an emerging profile. A systematic review of herb-induced
liver injury across all traditions counted 936 published cases involving 79 herbs or
products ([Ballotin 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34307603/)).

### Reports of poisoning rather than medicinal use

Three further plants on this site appear in the literature through poisonings, not through
use at any medicinal dose. Children in western Uttar Pradesh who ate the beans of
[Kasamarda](/herb/kasamarda/) (*Cassia occidentalis*) developed a fatal
hepatomyoencephalopathy; a case-control study found an odds ratio of 12.9 for bean eating
([Vashishtha 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17704552/); further cases in
[Chhapola 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29962748/) and
[Getsuwan 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33227120/)). Infants who swallowed 10 to
30 mL of clove oil ([Laung](/herb/laung/)) developed severe liver and kidney injury within a
day, which LiverTox rates
[C in overdose](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551727/) while finding no injury at
ordinary doses. Neem seed oil ([Neem](/herb/neem/)) given to infants has caused a Reye-like
syndrome with encephalopathy and liver involvement
([Sinniah 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7081175/);
[Lai 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2259944/)).

### Herbs LiverTox has reviewed and rates unlikely

For balance: LiverTox has also reviewed several Ayurvedic herbs and rates them E, unlikely
to cause clinically apparent liver injury, having found no convincing published case:
[Bacopa](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK603563/) ([Brahmi](/herb/brahmi/)),
[fenugreek](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548826/) ([Methi](/herb/methi/)),
[Boswellia serrata](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK563692/)
([Shallaki](/herb/shallaki/)),
[licorice](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK590484/) ([Yashtimadhu](/herb/yashtimadhu/))
and [berberine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK564659/), the main alkaloid of
[Daruharidra](/herb/daruharidra/) though not the plant itself.
[Tribulus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK583201/) ([Gokshura](/herb/gokshura/)) is
rated E with an asterisk: it poisons grazing animals, but human injury is unverified.

## How to read these reports

**Association is not proof.** A case report records that a person took a product and then
developed liver injury, after other causes were looked for. Causality scores such as RUCAM
grade how well the timing, the recovery on stopping, and the exclusion of other causes fit.
In the Giloy study, two-thirds of patients scored "probable" and one-third "possible"
([Kulkarni 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35037744/)); in the turmeric pathology
series, eight of eleven were probable ([Papke 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39381846/)).
No trial could settle the question, and none will be run.

**The product is often not just the herb.** Four of the five ashwagandha patients in the
Iceland and US series were taking other supplements, and in one case rhodiola was a possible
cause alongside ashwagandha ([Björnsson 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31991029/)).
Most of the turmeric cases in the literature involved another medicine too
([Lombardi 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32656820/)). The early Garcinia cases were
multi-ingredient weight-loss products
([LiverTox](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548087/)). In the Giloy study, two of six
retrieved samples had mercury, arsenic or lead above prescribed limits and some contained
industrial solvents ([Kulkarni 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35037744/)). A 2026
Brazilian series found a product labelled *Curcuma longa* with negligible turmeric
constituents and a weight-loss product with undeclared synthetic drugs
([Góis 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42491527/)). The reverse also holds: in the
Indian ashwagandha series and several of the US and Icelandic cases the product was tested
and contained what the label said and nothing else
([Philips 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37756041/)).

**Species disputes get settled by testing.** When the Giloy reports appeared, it was argued
that patients had taken *Tinospora crispa*, a related species with its own published
hepatitis cases ([Langrand 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24867504/);
[Huang 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670256/)). The Mumbai group retrieved the
plant material its patients had used and identified it as *T. cordifolia* by morphology,
chromatography and DNA barcoding ([Nagral 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36950495/)).
This site's page on [why one herb has several botanical
names](/choosing/one-herb-several-names/) explains why such disputes arise.

**Existing liver disease changes the stakes.** All four deaths in the Giloy study, the three
deaths in the Indian ashwagandha series, and most of the fatal Tinospora cases that LiverTox
describes were in people with chronic liver disease, several of whom did not know they had
it ([Kulkarni 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35037744/);
[Philips 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37756041/)). LiverTox says plainly that
ashwagandha should be avoided in cirrhosis or advanced chronic liver disease
([LiverTox: Ashwagandha](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/)).

**Some of it looks like autoimmunity.** Giloy injury came with autoantibodies and biopsy
features of autoimmune hepatitis, and the authors suggest it can unmask silent autoimmune
liver disease ([Nagral 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34230786/)). Seven of ten US
turmeric patients carried HLA-B*35:01, an allele also linked to green tea extract and
Garcinia injury, at an allele frequency of 0.45 against 0.06 in the population
([Halegoua-DeMarzio 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36252717/)). An idiosyncratic,
immune-mediated injury in a susceptible minority fits what is seen better than a poison that
harms everyone who takes enough.

## Multi-herb and heavy-metal products

Rasa shastra preparations deliberately contain processed metals and minerals, and the
[Ayurvedic Formulary of India sets no heavy-metal
limits](/choosing/reading-a-formulation-label/). What has been published about them is
mostly about lead, and mostly not about the liver.

- **Content.** 14 of 70 Ayurvedic products bought in Boston shops contained lead, mercury
  or arsenic ([Saper 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15598918/)). Of 193 products
  bought online, 20.7% contained a detectable metal, with no difference between US-made and
  Indian-made; rasa shastra products were more likely to (40.6% against 17.1%) and carried
  far higher mercury, and every metal-containing product exceeded at least one daily-intake
  standard ([Saper 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18728265/)). In an Iowa poisoning
  cluster, lead was found in 65% of 252 product samples
  ([Mikulski 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29528276/)).
- **Lead poisoning.** 12 adult cases across five US states, 2000 to 2003
  ([CDC 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15241300/)); 6 pregnant women in New York
  with blood lead of 16 to 64 µg/dL from products containing up to 2.4% lead
  ([CDC 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22914225/)); 46 of 115 screened members of
  one Ayurveda community with elevated blood lead
  ([Breeher 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843124/)); and, across ten years of
  New York City poisoning investigations, higher blood lead in rasa shastra users than in
  users of other remedies ([Hore 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36179284/)). Lead
  harms blood and the nervous system first; the anaemia in these patients was more severe
  than in occupational lead poisoning
  ([Kales 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17599022/)).
- **Kidney.** Anuric kidney failure in a 2-year-old after chronic intake of a mercury-containing
  Ayurvedic medicine ([Sathe 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23960350/)); lead
  poisoning from an Ayurvedic product in a patient with chronic kidney disease
  ([Prakash 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19384331/)).
- **Liver.** Of 33 south Indian patients with severe liver injury attributed to Ayurvedic
  and herbal medicines, five of the 27 followed with a biopsy died, and arsenic and mercury
  in the products they had taken were significantly associated with death
  ([Philips 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29476406/)). In 386 products retrieved
  from 91 patients at the same centre, mercury exceeded WHO limits in 34%, cadmium in 25%,
  arsenic in 21% and lead in 14%; 40% of patients had acute-on-chronic liver failure, with
  39% mortality in that group ([Philips 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41890551/)).
  Chronic arsenic from Ayurvedic preparations has been reported with non-cirrhotic portal
  hypertension, in an 11-year-old on eight preparations containing 5 to 248 mg/L arsenic
  ([Khandpur 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18477160/)) and in a 1979 series where
  two patients had taken bhasmas ([Datta 1979](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/468068/)).

The pages here for [Naga Bhasma](/herb/naga-bhasma/) (lead), [Haratala
Bhasma](/herb/haratala-bhasma/) and [Rasamanikya](/herb/rasamanikya/) (arsenic),
[Tamra Bhasma](/herb/tamra-bhasma/) (copper) and
[Arogyavardhini Vati](/formulation/arogyavardhini-vati/) (mercury and copper) report
animal studies in which correctly prepared material showed no toxicity at the usual dose.
Those studies test a preparation made by the method; the surveys above tested what was
actually on sale, and the two are not the same question. In the Giloy study, the authors
could not separate the herb from the metals and solvents some retrieved products also
contained, and said so ([Kulkarni 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35037744/)).

## If you suspect liver injury

Stop the product and see a doctor promptly if you notice yellowing of the eyes or skin, dark
urine, pale stools, itching, pain or tenderness under the right ribs, nausea, loss of
appetite or tiredness out of proportion to anything else going on. In the published series
the first signs came weeks to months after starting, so a product begun long ago is not
cleared by the delay.

Take the packaging with you, and name every product you have taken: herbs, supplements,
powders from a practitioner, teas, and anything bought online. Several of the cases above
were diagnosed late because the patient did not think of a herbal product as a medicine
([Bottenberg 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17726067/)). Most patients in these
reports recovered once the product was stopped, and that is also the first thing the doctor
will want to know you have done.

Anyone who already has liver disease, or a diagnosed autoimmune condition, has the strongest
reason to discuss any of these herbs with their doctor before taking them, because the
serious outcomes in the Indian series were concentrated in those patients.

## What this page does not do

It does not say that any herb is safe, or that any herb is dangerous. It reports what has
been published, with the number of patients and the authors' own caveats, so that the
strength of each link can be judged. It does not estimate how often injury happens, because
the denominator, the number of people taking each herb, is unknown. It does not give doses
or say what amount is safe; the reports do not support such a figure. It does not test
products, so it cannot say whether a particular packet contains what its label says. And it
is a reading of the literature on 26 October 2026, not medical advice for any one person:
the FAQ and the section above say when to see a doctor, and the doctor, not this page,
decides what to do next.
