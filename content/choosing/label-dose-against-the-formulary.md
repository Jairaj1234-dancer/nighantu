The Ayurvedic Formulary of India states a dose for the preparations it defines, and so does every
manufacturer selling them. Nobody has put the two side by side. On the <strong>129 product pages
where they can be compared</strong>, across 27 preparations and 22 companies, <strong>29 state the
formulary figure exactly</strong>, 51 overlap it, 15 sit entirely above and 14 entirely below. A
further 10 label ranges contain the formulary range, 5 sit inside it, and 5 meet it at a single
value without overlapping at all.

<p class="lede">
What follows compares two published figures. It is not a safety assessment, it does not say any

## The thing worth knowing before any table

Most label doses cannot be compared to the formulary at all, and the reasons divide into one that
is nobody's fault and one that is a choice.

Of 260 product pages where the formulary entry and the label both stated an amount, only 132 were
comparable and **128 were not**. On **21** of those 128, the formulary's figure is the weight of raw
drug used to *prepare* a decoction while the label states the volume *swallowed*. Those are
different quantities, and Dasamula Kvatha Curna is the clearest case: the formulary says 48 g, which
is the coarse powder you boil, and a ready-made kashayam page saying 15 to 20 ml is not a smaller
dose of the same thing. No comparison is drawn there, and that is a property of the formulary rather
than a failing of the company.

On **83** pages the reason is different, and it is now by far the commonest one. The label counts
tablets and never states what one tablet weighs. A company selling a classical preparation in tablet
form, naming the formulary entry it follows, and never printing a milligram figure per tablet has
published a product that cannot be checked against the standard it claims, by anyone, including a
pharmacist. That is a disclosure choice rather than an accident of the formulary, and widening this
survey from 7 companies to 22 did not dilute it: it grew from 24 pages to 83, which is 65% of
everything that could not be compared.

A further 13 state only a household measure with no gram or millilitre equivalent of their own.
Converting a teaspoon to grams would be our arithmetic rather than the manufacturer's statement, so
those are left uncompared too. 4 state no adult amount at all.

**Patanjali Ayurved states the formulary figure verbatim more often than any other company here.**
Eleven of its seventeen comparable pages give exactly the formulary range: 12 to 24 ml for
Abhayarishta, Arjunarishta, Ashokarishta, Kutajarishta and Saraswatarishta, 1 to 3 g for Sitopaladi
Churna, 3 to 6 g for Avipattikar Churna, 250 to 500 mg for Arogyavardhini Vati. This was not the
expected result, and widening the survey from 7 companies to 22 did not displace it. It is the
second time this project has found that the segment a company belongs to does not predict what it
publishes.

**Sitaram Ayurveda sits entirely above the formulary on 8 of its 11 pages**, stating 25 to 30 ml for
every one of its arishtas against a formulary figure of 12 to 24 ml for each. The uniformity is the
point: several preparations with the same formulary dose and one house figure above it, applied
consistently.

**Shree Dhootapapeshwar states 5 to 10 g for its churnas**, against formulary figures of 1 to 2 g
for Hingvastaka, 1 to 3 g for Sitopaladi and 3 g for Talisadi. Its own label does the conversion
for you and says so: "1 tsf. to 2 tsf. (5 to 10 gm.)". So the house unit is the teaspoon and the
gram figure is theirs, which is why these pages are comparable at all while the teaspoon-only pages
of other companies are not.

**The three largest contributors after Patanjali are companies no previous survey here had read.**
Tansukh Herbals, Shree Dhanwantri Herbals and Swadeshi Ayurved supply 38 of the 129 rows between
them, and all three sit mostly in the overlapping band rather than at the formulary figure or far
from it. Swadeshi Ayurved has no exact match on any of its 12 pages.

**Kerala Ayurveda overlaps on five of six**, stating 15 to 25 ml where the formulary says 12 to 24,
and it is the only company in this set that publishes a separate children's dose alongside the
adult one on the same page.

## What was withheld, and why

Three of the 132 comparable claims were refused by the adversarial check and are not in the table
above. All three would have been wrong in print.

The first compared a Saraswatarishta figure of 3 to 12 ml to the formulary's 12 to 24 ml. The check
found that 3 to 12 ml is exactly the range Kerala Ayurveda publishes as its **children's** dose for
the same preparation, that the Maharishi page states no population at all, and that a second
Maharishi page for the same preparation gives a different figure again. Treating it as an adult
dose was unsupported, so no relation is published for it.

The second compared a Sitopaladi Churna figure of 3 to 6 g to the formulary's 1 to 3 g. The label
states that amount as a **daily** total while the formulary figure is per administration, so the
two are not the same measure. Taken twice daily the label's figure sits inside the formulary range;
taken once it sits above it, and the page does not say which.

The third concerned Curoveda Herbals and is a different kind of error. Its Triphala Churna states
1 to 3 g against a formulary 3 to 6 g, and the comparison had called that "overlapping". The two
ranges share only the single value 3; every other amount on the label lies below the formulary
minimum. The check refused the word rather than the numbers.

All three are published here rather than quietly dropped, because the count of what a measurement
refused to say is part of what the measurement is worth.

## How the relation is decided, after the third refusal changed it

That third refusal was about vocabulary, and checking how far it reached found the problem was not
confined to one row: of 71 rows then labelled "overlapping", nine shared exactly one value with the
formulary range, and several were not overlap at all but containment. A label stating 125 mg against
a formulary range of 125 to 250 mg sits inside it.

So the relation is no longer judged. Reading an amount out of a sentence is a judgement and is still
done one preparation at a time; comparing two numeric ranges is arithmetic, and is now computed from
the two figures after converting both to a common unit. That gives eight relations instead of four,
which is what the data actually contains: identical, within, contains, overlapping, touching above,
touching below, entirely above, entirely below.

The two methods disagreed on 20 of the 129 published rows, and the arithmetic is published in every
case. The disagreements are in the dataset so they can be checked rather than taken on trust.

One of them went the other way and is worth recording, because it is the only case where the
arithmetic was wrong and the judgement pass was right. Hind Herbal Pharmacy states 2 to 5 g for
Arogyavardhini Vati against a formulary 250 to 500 mg. The first version of the arithmetic compared
the bare numbers and concluded the label sat entirely **below** the formulary. It is eight to ten
times above it. The agent had read the units and the arithmetic had not, and the row now converts
both sides before comparing and refuses to compare a mass against a volume at all.

## What this does not measure

It does not measure whether any dose is correct, safe, or appropriate for any person. It does not
measure the strength of any preparation, which can differ legitimately between manufacturers and
would change what a given volume contains. It covers only preparations where the formulary states a
dose and where the manufacturer states one in a comparable form, which is 129 pages: a small
fraction of what these companies sell. It reads product pages and not labels, and a printed label
may state something the website does not.

**And the coverage is limited by our own instrument, not only by what companies publish.** Products
are found by matching the 101 formulary names against a product page's URL, so a company selling
Abhayarishta at `/product/12345` is invisible to it. That is measured rather than assumed: of 53
surveyed hosts whose URLs named no formulary preparation at all, 34 could be sampled further, and on
**7 of those 34 a page title named a preparation the URL did not**. Lama Pharmaceuticals sells
Sariwadyasav and Triphala Guggulu, Everest Ayurveda sells Amrutharishta, SKM sells Chyavanaprasham,
and none of those URLs say so.

So the right reading of a company's absence from this page is "we did not find its formulary
products", never "it has none". The miss rate is roughly one host in five, the full test is in
[/manufacturer-register.json](/manufacturer-register.json)'s companion check, and the regional
spellings are a large part of why: Abhayarist, Sariwadyasav, Khadiradi Bati, Kanchnar Guggul.

And it rests on a transcription. The formulary figures come from this project's own reading of the
Ayurvedic Formulary of India, cited to part and entry so anyone can check them against the book.
