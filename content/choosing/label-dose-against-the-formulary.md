The Ayurvedic Formulary of India states a dose for the preparations it defines, and so does every
manufacturer selling them. Nobody has put the two side by side. On the <strong>60 product pages
where they can be compared</strong>, across 24 preparations and 7 companies, <strong>18 state the
formulary figure exactly</strong>, 24 overlap it, 13 sit entirely above it and 5 entirely below.

<p class="lede">
What follows compares two published figures. It is not a safety assessment, it does not say any
dose is wrong, and it cannot tell you what to take. A manufacturer may differ from the formulary
for good reasons, and the formulary itself prefaces its doses as general guidance.
</p>

## The thing worth knowing before any table

Most label doses cannot be compared to the formulary at all, and the reasons divide into one that
is nobody's fault and one that is a choice.

Of 118 product pages where the formulary entry and the label both stated an amount, only 62 were
comparable and **56 were not**. On **17** of those 56, the formulary's figure is the weight of raw drug used to
*prepare* a decoction while the label states the volume *swallowed*. Those are different
quantities, and Dasamula Kvatha Curna is the clearest case: the formulary says 48 g, which is the
coarse powder you boil, and a ready-made kashayam page saying 15 to 20 ml is not a smaller dose of
the same thing. No comparison is drawn there, and that is a property of the formulary rather than a
failing of the company.

On **24** pages the reason is different. The label counts tablets and never states what one tablet
weighs. A company selling a classical preparation in tablet form, naming the formulary entry it
follows, and never printing a milligram figure per tablet has published a product that cannot be
checked against the standard it claims, by anyone, including a pharmacist. That is a disclosure
choice and it is the single commonest reason this comparison cannot be made.

A further 7 state only a household measure with no gram or millilitre equivalent of their own.
Converting a teaspoon to grams would be our arithmetic rather than the manufacturer's statement, so
those are left uncompared too.

**Patanjali Ayurved states the formulary figure verbatim more often than every classical pharmacy
here combined.** Eleven of its seventeen comparable pages give exactly the formulary range: 12 to
24 ml for Abhayarishta, Arjunarishta, Ashokarishta, Kutajarishta and Saraswatarishta, 1 to 3 g for
Sitopaladi Churna, 3 to 6 g for Avipattikar Churna, 250 to 500 mg for Arogyavardhini Vati. This was
not the expected result. It is the second time this survey has found that the segment a company
belongs to does not predict what it publishes.

**Sitaram Ayurveda states 25 to 30 ml for all six of its arishtas**, against a formulary figure of
12 to 24 ml for each of them. The uniformity is the point: six preparations with the same formulary
dose and one house figure above it, applied consistently.

**Shree Dhootapapeshwar states 5 to 10 g for its churnas**, against formulary figures of 1 to 2 g
for Hingvastaka, 1 to 3 g for Sitopaladi and 3 g for Talisadi. Its own label does the conversion
for you and says so: "1 tsf. to 2 tsf. (5 to 10 gm.)". So the house unit is the teaspoon and the
gram figure is theirs, which is why these pages are comparable at all while Maharishi's
teaspoon-only arishta pages are not.

**Kerala Ayurveda overlaps on five of six**, stating 15 to 25 ml where the formulary says 12 to 24,
and it is the only company in this set that publishes a separate children's dose alongside the
adult one on the same page.

## What was withheld, and why

Two of the 62 comparable claims were refused by the adversarial check and are not in the table
above. Both concerned Maharishi Ayurveda and both would have been wrong in print.

The first compared a Saraswatarishta figure of 3 to 12 ml to the formulary's 12 to 24 ml. The check
found that 3 to 12 ml is exactly the range Kerala Ayurveda publishes as its **children's** dose for
the same preparation, that the Maharishi page states no population at all, and that a second
Maharishi page for the same preparation gives a different figure again. Treating it as an adult
dose was unsupported, so no relation is published for it.

The second compared a Sitopaladi Churna figure of 3 to 6 g to the formulary's 1 to 3 g. The label
states that amount as a **daily** total while the formulary figure is per administration, so the
two are not the same measure. Taken twice daily the label's figure sits inside the formulary range;
taken once it sits above it, and the page does not say which.

Both are published here rather than quietly dropped, because the count of what a measurement
refused to say is part of what the measurement is worth.

## What this does not measure

It does not measure whether any dose is correct, safe, or appropriate for any person. It does not
measure the strength of any preparation, which can differ legitimately between manufacturers and
would change what a given volume contains. It covers only preparations where the formulary states a
dose and where the manufacturer states one in a comparable form, which is 60 pages: a small
fraction of what these companies sell. It reads product pages and not labels, and a printed label
may state something the website does not.

And it rests on a transcription. The formulary figures come from this project's own reading of the
Ayurvedic Formulary of India, cited to part and entry so anyone can check them against the book.
