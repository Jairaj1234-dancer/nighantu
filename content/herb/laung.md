## Key Phytochemical Constituents

- Oleanolic acid
- [Vanillin](/glossary/compounds-t-z/#vanillin)

## How does it work?

- Eugenol inhibits COX-2 and iNOS expression, reducing prostaglandin E2 and nitric oxide production for anti-inflammatory and [analgesic](/reference/analgesic/) effects
- In preclinical (cell and animal) studies, [anticancer](/reference/anticancer/) activity through multiple pathways: induction of apoptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction, cell cycle arrest at G2/M phase, and inhibition of NF-kB-mediated survival signaling
- [Antimicrobial](/reference/antimicrobial/) mechanism via disruption of bacterial cell membrane integrity, inhibition of protein synthesis, and interference with quorum sensing
- Anesthetic/analgesic effect through inhibition of voltage-gated sodium channels and TRPV1 receptor modulation in nociceptive neurons
- [Antioxidant](/reference/antioxidant/) activity through phenolic compound-mediated free radical scavenging and upregulation of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant [enzymes](/glossary/compounds-d-g/#enzymes)

## Which traditional uses are supported by research?

*These summaries come from the source notes, which do not name the studies behind them.*

- Toothache and dental pain: traditionally used for toothache and dental pain. Its component eugenol is used in dental practice as an analgesic and antiseptic.
- Oral infections and wound care: traditionally used for oral infections and wound care. In vitro and clinical studies have reported broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, including against multidrug-resistant bacteria.
- Flatulence, colic and nausea: traditionally used as a digestive aid for flatulence, colic and nausea. Pharmacological studies have reported [antispasmodic](/glossary/pharmacology/#antispasmodic) and carminative effects.
- Joint and muscle pain: traditionally used for joint and muscle pain. Studies have reported COX-2 inhibition and reduced inflammatory markers.
- Cough and sore throat: traditionally used for cough and sore throat. Studies have reported [expectorant](/reference/expectorant/) and antimicrobial activity.

## Recent safety updates

- WHO classifies eugenol as a GRAS (generally recognized as safe) substance with acceptable daily intake (ADI) of maximum 2.5 mg/kg body weight
- High-dose eugenol can cause hepatotoxicity through quinone methide intermediate formation by peroxidases; cytotoxicity also linked to membrane disruption due to high lipophilicity and uncoupling of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation
- Topical clove oil may cause contact dermatitis and mucosal irritation; oral high doses (40 mg/kg eugenol) can impair kidney function; avoid concurrent use with anticoagulant medications due to antiplatelet activity

## Dosage forms and preparation

**Dosage Forms:** Churna (powder), Essential oil, Tablets, Capsules, Toothache drops, Mouthwash, Lozenges, Taila (medicated oil), Topical gel

**Standard Dosage:** 1-3 g clove powder per day; 1-5 drops essential oil (diluted) for topical dental use; 250-500 mg extract capsules twice daily

**Optimal Timing:** After meals for digestive support; as needed for dental pain relief; morning and evening for oral hygiene products

**Storage:** Store whole cloves in airtight containers at room temperature. Essential oil in amber glass, tightly sealed, below 25 deg C. Ground powder rapidly loses potency - store in nitrogen-flushed, foil-lined sachets. Protect all forms from light and moisture.

**Marker Compounds:** Eugenol, Eugenol acetate, Beta-caryophyllene, Alpha-humulene, Gallic acid, [Kaempferol](/reference/kaempferol/), Oleanolic acid, Vanillin
